Coastal Towns Switch On Community Solar Microgrids

Three harbour communities now generate and share their own power, and residents say the lights stayed on during last month's storms.

By

Amara Okafor
12 October 2026

Residents of three coastal towns began drawing electricity from a shared solar microgrid this month, a project organisers say could become a model for communities that regularly lose grid power in bad weather.

The network links rooftop panels on schools, fishing cooperatives and homes to a central battery store. When the main grid fails, the microgrid disconnects automatically and keeps essential buildings running.

How the microgrid works

Each participating building generates power during the day. Surplus energy is stored in a battery bank beside the harbour and shared with neighbours who have no panels of their own.

Engineers describe the system as "islanding": in an outage, the microgrid separates from the national network and operates independently until service returns.

Key features of the scheme include:

  • 40 rooftop installations across three towns
  • A shared battery store holding enough energy for about 12 hours of essential use
  • Automatic switchover when the main grid fails

What it means for residents

Organisers say households in the scheme will pay roughly a fifth less for electricity than the regional average, with profits reinvested locally.

During the storm we were the only street with lights on. People came to charge their phones and check on each other.

— Tunde Adeyemi, resident of Marina Row

Not everyone is convinced. Some residents worry about maintenance costs once the initial grant funding ends, and the project team has promised to publish annual accounts. Read the International Renewable Energy Agency's overview of community energy for background on similar schemes worldwide.

What happens next

The project team plans to add six more towns by the end of next year. A public meeting to discuss expansion is scheduled for 5 November at the Marina Community Hall.
